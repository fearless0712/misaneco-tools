Simple automation for a small hair or beauty salon

If you run a small hair salon, nail bar or barbershop, the work does not stop when the last client leaves. There are bookings to confirm, notes to write up, stock to count and takings to add up. It can easily add an hour to the end of the day.

This article splits that admin into seven jobs. For each one you get the problem, a simple way to handle it, and one first step. It does not recommend any particular product. The idea is to start with tools you probably already have: a calendar, a spreadsheet and your email.

1. Online booking

The problem. Bookings arrive by phone, text and social media messages. Keeping them in one place is hard, and double bookings are a worry.

A simple approach. Google Calendar has a feature called appointment schedules. You create a booking page, and clients pick a free slot.

According to Google's help page, a few things are worth knowing:

  • You create an appointment schedule in Google Calendar on a computer. You cannot create one in the Calendar app for Android, iPhone or iPad.
  • It works with a personal Google Account or a Workspace account. Some features need an eligible Google Workspace or Google One subscription.
  • The booking form asks for first name, last name and email address. You can add more fields.
  • You can set up confirmation emails, and you can send up to 5 reminders. You cannot edit the text of the reminder emails.
  • The default minimum lead time is 4 hours, so same-day slots may not show up right after you set it up.
  • Taking payment through the booking page needs a connected Stripe account.

First step. Even without a booking page, pick one diary as the single source of truth. Every booking, whatever way it came in, goes into that diary the same day.

2. Client records and visit history

The problem. Finding what you did last time means digging through paper cards or scrolling old messages.

A simple approach. Use a spreadsheet with one row per visit. Useful columns are a client ID, visit date, service, staff member and notes. A client ID instead of full personal details keeps the main sheet lighter on sensitive information. Keep the names and phone numbers in a separate, more restricted place.

Agree a standard notes format, so any stylist can pick up a client. Write the notes straight after the visit, while you still remember.

First step. Limit yourself to five columns. You can add more later.

Client records are personal data, so please read the privacy section below.

3. Rebooking reminders

The problem. You want regulars to come back, but remembering who is due is a job nobody has time for.

A simple approach. Add two columns to your client sheet: "last visit" and "usual gap in weeks". A simple formula adds the two and gives a "due around" date. Sort by that date and you have a list of people to contact this month. You can also highlight clients you have not seen for 90 days.

The easiest rebooking habit costs nothing: offer the next appointment at checkout.

Before you message people, check that they are happy to be contacted. Marketing messages have their own rules in many countries (see the privacy section).

First step. Add the "due around" column and look at the list once a week.

4. Retail stock

The problem. The shampoo or nail polish you sell runs out just when someone asks for it.

A simple approach. A stock sheet with columns for item, quantity on hand, reorder point and supplier. Turn on conditional formatting to highlight any item that falls below its reorder point. Count on a fixed day each week, and give one person the job of placing orders. A "running low" list by the till also helps.

First step. List the five products you run out of most often.

5. Daily takings report

The problem. Adding up receipts and card slips after closing is slow and error-prone.

A simple approach. One row per day: total sales, number of transactions, and the split between cash and card. Add monthly and weekday totals with formulas, and a chart comparing this month with last month. If your till or booking system can export a CSV file, you can paste in that export and skip some typing.

Write down your close-up steps and pick three numbers to watch each week. A routine matters more than a clever spreadsheet.

First step. Enter the day's figures before you leave, every day for a week.

6. Staff rota and timesheets

The problem. Collecting availability and building the weekly rota eats time. Mistakes show up as gaps in cover.

A simple approach. Use a rota sheet with days across the top and staff down the side. Set the number of staff you need per shift and highlight gaps. Add a total of hours per person, which doubles as a simple timesheet. For availability, a short online form that asks for name, week and times available feeds neatly into a sheet.

Agree three rules in writing: a weekly availability deadline, how swaps get approved, and the day the rota is published.

First step. Fix a deadline for availability and publish the rota on the same day each week.

7. Review and enquiry replies

The problem. Writing each reply from scratch takes longer than it should, and the tone can vary between staff.

A simple approach. Write templates for the messages you send most often: thanking someone for a review, answering a price question, and giving opening hours.

Gmail can store these. Google's help page says you can only turn on and use templates from Gmail on a computer. In Settings, open the Advanced tab, enable Templates and save. Then write a message, choose More options, Templates, "Save draft as template". To use one, open a new message and insert it from the same menu. You can also set up a filter so a chosen template is sent as an automatic reply. Note that a deleted template cannot be recovered.

Keep it human. Add the client's name and one personal line before you send. Decide who replies and when, and pass complaints to the owner.

First step. Write templates for your three most common questions.

Client records are personal data

A client's name, phone number, appointment history and treatment notes are personal data. Rules differ by country, and this is not legal advice. Here is what the official bodies say, in brief.

United Kingdom. The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) has security advice for small organisations. It suggests measures such as strong passwords, firewalls and anti-virus software, training staff to spot suspicious emails, and not holding data for longer than you need it. It says "the more sensitive the data, the more safeguards you need to have in place." It also says you must have appropriate security measures in place when sending bulk emails. The ICO's small-organisation pages also link to a separate section on direct marketing.

Australia. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says most small businesses (annual turnover of $3 million or less) are not covered by the Privacy Act, but turnover is not the only test. For example, health service providers are covered regardless of turnover. Even for businesses that are not covered, the OAIC recommends you protect any personal information you hold.

Canada. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (OPC) lists ten fair information principles that businesses covered by PIPEDA must follow, including consent, limiting collection and safeguards. Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have their own provincial laws that are deemed substantially similar.

A few common-sense habits follow from all of this:

  • Collect only what you need. Do not record details you will never use.
  • Decide who can open the client file and where it is stored. Review that list from time to time.
  • Use strong, unique passwords and turn on two-step sign-in where it is offered.
  • Delete old records you no longer need.
  • Ask clients before you add them to a marketing list.

Where to start

You do not need to do all seven at once. Choose the job that costs you the most time, and try only its first step. A few habits keep things from getting messy later:

  • Keep one place for each type of record.
  • Agree how dates and service names are written, so totals work.
  • Spend five minutes a week checking the sheets still make sense.
